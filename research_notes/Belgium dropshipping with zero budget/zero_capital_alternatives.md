## 1. Demand and pricing for AI-generated video services (AI UGC ads, product videos, AI commercials, music videos, short-form)

### Takeaway
Demand is real and growing: by 2025 most ad buyers were using or planning to use GenAI for video ads, and Malt saw European AI-project demand outgrow the supply of AI-skilled freelancers. Prices are split in two. Commodity "AI UGC" clips sell for $5-$100 on Fiverr, and AI tools make them for $3-$25 each. Directed, brand-grade work sells for roughly $150-$600 per ad, $1,200-$4,000 per batch and $2,500-$8,000/month on retainer. A solo creative can win by being a director rather than a prompt operator: concept, script and hooks, brand consistency, editing, and accountability for performance. Consumer distrust of obvious AI ads is the main risk.

### Inferences
- There are two markets. (a) Commodity AI UGC and "talking avatar" ads, priced at $10-$100 on Fiverr and competing with $3-$25 self-serve tools. This is a race to the bottom, and a solo person should avoid competing on price there. (b) Directed "AI commercials", product films and hook-tested ad batches for SMEs and DTC brands, priced at $150-$600 per ad or $1,200-$4,000 per batch, plus retainers. This person's "AI Film Director" positioning fits (b).
- The anchor for Belgian SMEs is a traditional shoot (€1,000-€4,000 for a testimonial, €3,500+ for a brand film in NL, or €350-€480/day for a Belgian motion designer). An AI product or brand film at €500-€1,500 is cheaper than any shoot and still pays far better than Fiverr.
- What separates winners from the crowd (inferred from the adoption uses and the consumer-trust data): volume of variations for testing (versioning is the #1 GenAI use, at 42%), brand and character consistency, real-product accuracy, and a craft finish that does not read as cheap AI. Selling "performance creative" (hooks × variations) rather than "a video" matches what buyers say they use GenAI for.
- The consumer-backlash data argues for hybrid work (real product footage or photos plus AI scenes) and clear disclosure for realistic people. It also suggests avoiding fully synthetic "fake testimonials", which are also the riskiest under the AI Act and consumer law (see §7).

### Gaps
- No platform-wide price index for "AI video" on Upwork, Fiverr, Contra or Malt was found. All per-video figures are vendor-blog estimates or single listings.
- No Malt Belgium barometer for video or motion design was reachable. Only French and UK averages and individual Belgian listings are available.
- No pricing data for AI music videos was found in any query.
- No Belgian or EU-specific survey of SME adoption of AI video was found. The IAB data is US-centric.
- No hard data on how saturated the market is (number of AI video sellers over time) was found. Saturation is inferred from many near-identical Fiverr gigs and very low price points.

## 2. Web design / landing pages / animated websites for Belgian SMEs, and website + video bundles

### Takeaway
Belgian SMEs typically budget €2,000-€5,000 for a quality business website (overall range €500-€15,000+). Belgian freelance web developers bill about €30-€80/hour against €50-€150 for agencies, and recurring hosting and maintenance adds another €250-€1,300/year. This is a proven local market with recurring revenue potential. A bundle of an animated site plus a cinematic AI hero video is plausible as a differentiator, but no source documents bundle pricing.

### Cited Findings
- A Belgian 2026 price guide: a website costs €500 to €15,000+; most SMEs budget €2,000-€5,000 for a quality corporate site; freelance web developers charge €30-€80/hour and agencies €50-€150/hour; hosting costs €50-€300/year and maintenance €200-€1,000/year. The source is a vendor that sells web development, so it may be skewed upward — [LUNIDEV](https://lunidev.com/nl/blog/website-kosten-2026-complete-prijsgids-belgie)
- A Dutch directory lists brand-style design at €200-€1,200 and advises getting 3-4 quotes — [Trustoo](https://trustoo.nl/noord-brabant/son-en-breugel/webdesign/)
- Older French and Belgian client project listings show website budgets of €300-€1,000 (small organisations, ASBLs) — [Graphiste.com](https://graphiste.com/projects/77349-web-design)
- The average employed webdesigner salary in Belgium is about €2,486 gross/month (2023 data). This is employment pay, not a freelance rate — [Indeed BE](https://be.indeed.com/career/webdesigner/salaries)
- E-commerce angle: Fiverr Shopify store builds start at $120 (seller with 1k+ reviews, 4.9★) and $200 (top-rated seller, one-product store with landing page) — [Fiverr dropshipping](https://www.fiverr.com/gigs/dropshipping)

### Inferences
- With Claude and similar tools he can build animated sites quickly at almost no cost, so the business limit is sales, not delivery. A €1,500-€3,500 "cinematic site" package aimed at Belgian SMEs sits inside the typical €2,000-€5,000 budget, and maintenance or hosting at €20-€80/month (inside the cited €200-€1,000/year) builds recurring revenue.
- A website + AI hero film bundle (for example, a site plus a 20-30s brand film plus 5 social cutdowns) lets him compete on something other than hourly rate. Comparing the cited ranges, it could reasonably sit at €2,500-€5,000, but this is an inference, not a documented market price.
- Local SMEs (hospitality, real estate, salons, artisans, B2B services) care less about "AI" and more about looking premium and getting leads. That makes AI a cost advantage rather than the headline, which avoids the consumer-trust issues in §1.

### Gaps
- No Belgian freelance web-design rate survey (Malt BE barometer, Unizo or SBB) was reachable. The figures rest on one vendor guide.
- No data on demand specifically for animated or "award-style" sites among Belgian SMEs, and no data on bundle pricing.
- Belgian SME website penetration and replacement-cycle statistics were not researched.

## 3. "Selling shovels": ad creatives, product videos and store design for e-commerce and dropshipping sellers

### Takeaway
Selling services to dropshippers and e-commerce sellers is an established, high-volume model on Fiverr. Many sellers have 500-1,000+ reviews, which shows sustained demand. But prices are very low (ad creatives from $5-$75, stores from $120-$200) and gigs are nearly identical. It works as a fast way to get first reviews and cash. It is a poor fit for a premium "film director" brand unless he moves up to established DTC/Shopify brands with real ad budgets.

### Cited Findings
- Fiverr dropshipping video-ad gigs start at about $5-$75. One seller with 1k+ reviews and 4.8★ starts at $25; a 4.9★ seller with 529 reviews offers 3 ads from $60 ("video ad for your amazon product, dropshipping, aliexpress") — [Fiverr dropshipping videos](https://www.fiverr.com/gigs/dropshipping-videos); [Fiverr dropshipping ads](https://www.fiverr.com/gigs/dropshipping-ads); [Fiverr Shopify video ad](https://www.fiverr.com/gigs/shopify-video-ad)
- Shopify dropshipping store builds start at $120 (1k+ reviews, 4.9★) and $200 (top-rated, one-product store plus landing page). TikTok Shop setup and management add-ons cost $15-$75 — [Fiverr dropshipping](https://www.fiverr.com/gigs/dropshipping); [Fiverr TikTok dropshipping](https://www.fiverr.com/gigs/tiktok-dropshipping)
- In a test of Fiverr dropshipping ad creators, gigs started around $51, ranging from $20 to $100 — [WebsitePlanet](https://www.websiteplanet.com/blog/best-dropshipping-video-ad-creators/)
- Agency AI/UGC ad retainers for brands run $2,500-$8,000/month (guide), and agency UGC retainers $3,000-$25,000/month — [Motionbox](https://motionbox.io/blog/how-much-to-charge-clients-for-ai-ugc-ads); [Sparkiz](https://sparkiz.ai/blog/ugc-ads-cost/)
- Fiverr sellers keep 80% of each order; Upwork charges freelancers 0-15% per contract — [Genra](https://genra.ai/blog/ai-video-freelancer-business-guide)

### Inferences
- The model is proven: thousands of reviews at $25-$60 means thousands of orders. But net per order after Fiverr's 20% cut is tiny. At about $50 an order, €2,000/month needs roughly 45-50 orders.
- Dropshippers are the most price-sensitive, highest-churn buyers. They test many products and abandon most. Established Shopify/DTC brands doing €20k+/month in sales, which buy creatives continuously, are the better target for the same skill, at the $150-$600 per ad or $1,200-$4,000 per batch level from §1.
- A credible way in: offer "10 ad hooks / variations per product" packages to EU (including Benelux) Shopify brands, and use a few cheap Fiverr orders only to build a portfolio and reviews.

### Gaps
- No earnings data for top Fiverr sellers in these categories (Fiverr does not publish it). Only asking prices are visible.
- No data on churn or repeat-purchase rates of dropshipping clients.
- No EU-specific marketplace data (Malt or Contra demand for ad creatives).

## 4. Productized services and retainers: solo creatives at €5k-€20k/month with AI video or creative services

### Takeaway
No independently verified case of a solo AI-video creative earning €5k-€20k/month was found. Every example is self-reported, secondhand or hypothetical. This makes €5k/month look achievable with about 2-4 retainer clients. €20k/month appears to need a small team or agency model.

- The report should present €5k-€20k/month as possible but undocumented for solo AI video creatives. €1k-€3k/month within the first 3-6 months is the more defensible planning range (inferred from 1-3 small clients at the cited per-video and batch prices).

## 5. Other zero-capital models: digital products, print-on-demand, affiliate, a YouTube/TikTok channel, local lead-gen, festivals

### Takeaway
All of these can start at €0, but their typical outcomes are poor and slow compared with service work. Half of Notion templates with visible sales earned under $520 lifetime. Cheap prompt packs earn cents per sale. AI print-on-demand is saturated: AI designs are 12-15% of new Etsy/Redbubble listings, margins are a few dollars, and Etsy requires disclosure. YouTube demonetises mass-produced AI content. AI film festival prizes are large (Runway AIF Grand Prix $50k; Astana $450k Grand Prize) but work like a lottery. These are best as audience-building and portfolio side-assets that feed the service business, not as the main source of income.

## 6. How to get the first clients with €0: cold outreach, spec work, local networking, marketplaces, posting work

### Takeaway
Cold email typically gets 1-5% replies (Hunter: 4.5% average in 2025; Mailshake: 1-4% most common). LinkedIn gets roughly 25-30% connection acceptance and about 5-10% replies after connecting, at €0 cost. At these rates, a few hundred well-targeted contacts should produce a handful of conversations and probably the first paid project within weeks. Personalisation (specific triggers, showing a sample made for that brand) roughly doubles response. That makes spec work (a free 10-15s AI ad made for the prospect) the logical hook, but no documented "spec ad → client" case was found. Belgian networks such as Unizo and BNI exist, but BNI costs money (about $539-$699/year in the US; Belgian price unknown).

## 7. Legal/ethical considerations (EU AI Act Art. 50, copyright, client contracts, tool commercial terms) and AI video tool costs

### Takeaway
Since 2 August 2026, Article 50(4) of the AI Act requires deployers to clearly label realistic AI-generated "deepfake" image, audio or video content. The lighter "artistic/creative/fictional" regime probably does not cover ordinary ads. Fines reach €15M or 3% of turnover. Purely AI-generated output is unlikely to be copyrightable in the EU or Belgium, so contracts should assign "whatever rights exist", allocate risk and avoid promising exclusive ownership. Commercial use generally requires paid tool plans: the Dreamina/Seedance free tier and the Kling free tier reportedly bar commercial use. Entry paid plans cost about $10-$20/month each (Kling Standard $10; Dreamina about $15 after a $0.29 promo; Google AI Pro $19.99; Runway Standard $15). A €0 start is realistic with free trials and promos, and about €30-€60/month covers a lean professional stack.

### Cited Findings
**EU AI Act Article 50: timeline and obligations**
- The Commission published the final, voluntary Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content on 10 June 2026. The Commission and the AI Board found it adequate for Art. 50(2), (4) and (5) on 8-9 July 2026. Final Commission Guidelines on Art. 50 were also published — [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-code-practice-marking-and-labelling-ai-generated-content); [Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/7/eu-ai-act-commission-confirms-transparency-code-of-practice-as-adequate-and-publishes-final-version-of-its-guidelines-on-transparency-obligations); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102nbz0/transparency-obligations-for-ai-generated-content-the-code-of-practice-adequacy/); [Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content)
- Art. 50 obligations apply from 2 August 2026. Under the "AI Omnibus" grace period, the providers' machine-readable marking duty (Art. 50(2)) for generative systems already on the market is reportedly extended to 2 December 2026. Under the final Guidelines, content created before 2 August 2026 does not need a label. Text created earlier but published later is treated differently — [Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/7/eu-ai-act-commission-confirms-transparency-code-of-practice-as-adequate-and-publishes-final-version-of-its-guidelines-on-transparency-obligations); [Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content); [artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/transparency-rules-article-50/)
- Art. 50(4): deployers must clearly label deepfakes, and AI-generated text published on matters of public interest. Under the Code, a visual label should be "recognizable upon first exposure, not obscured by other elements, and remain visible for a sufficient period". Audio-only deepfakes need an audio warning at the start. A common EU "AI" icon (localised as KI or IA) has been proposed — [Peterka Partners](https://blog.peterkapartners.com/article-50-of-the-ai-act-in-practice-transparency-of-ai-systems-content-labeling-and-deepfakes/); [AIActo](https://www.aiacto.eu/en/blog/article-50-ai-act-transparence-deepfakes-contenu-ia); [European Compliance Suite](https://europeancompliancesuite.com/eu-ai-act-article-50-labelling/)
- Artistic carve-out (Commission FAQ): for "evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous" works, the duty is limited to disclosure "in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work". Disclosure can sit in credits, metadata or a description. It relaxes where the disclosure goes, but does not remove it — [Commission FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act); [Numonic](https://www.numonic.ai/blog/eu-ai-act-article-50-content-creators); [Nicfab](https://www.nicfab.eu/en/posts/ai-transparency-disclosure/)
- Advertising: commentators argue commercial marketing (product images, ad videos) does not qualify as artistic expression for the carve-out, unless it is "unmistakably fictional or satirical". Synthetic spokespeople and misleading AI product imagery usually count as deepfakes. Realistic misleading depictions of real people are never covered by the carve-out. These are interpretive views, not settled law — [Legalithm](https://www.legalithm.com/en/blog/ai-act-transparency-obligations-article-50-deepfake-labeling); [Fontvera](https://fontvera.eu/intelligence/ai-act-faq-deepfakes); [AIActBase](https://aiactbase.eu/deepfake-ai-content-labelling-requirements/); [Regulation-AI](https://www.regulation-ai.eu/en/transparency-obligations/)
- Penalties: commentators cite up to €15M or 3% of worldwide annual turnover. Sources conflict on whether SMEs face the lower or higher of the two. The Article 99 text was not verified — [Fontvera](https://fontvera.eu/intelligence/ai-act-faq-deepfakes); [Legalithm](https://www.legalithm.com/en/blog/ai-act-transparency-obligations-article-50-deepfake-labeling)
- The deepfake test depends on realistic resemblance to existing people, places or events. Not every AI image is caught — [AIActBase](https://aiactbase.eu/deepfake-ai-content-labelling-requirements/); [arXiv: "What constitutes a Deep Fake?"](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09961v2)
- Platform rules add their own disclosure duties: Etsy for AI designs, and YouTube for inauthentic AI content — [AIBusiness.vc](https://aibusiness.vc/solo/ai-art-print-on-demand); [BetaNews](https://betanews.com/2025/07/10/youtube-is-fighting-ai-slop-with-new-monetization-guidelines/)
